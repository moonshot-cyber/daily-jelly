"Hydrolysed collagen" means the collagen has been broken down — using enzymes — into fragments small enough for your body to actually absorb. That's it. It's not a premium ingredient, a special formulation, or a marketing distinction. It's the basic processing step that makes a collagen supplement work as a supplement rather than passing through you undigested. Nearly every collagen product sold in the UK states it on the label because nearly every collagen product does it.

## Why collagen needs to be hydrolysed in the first place

Collagen in its natural state is a large, tough, triple-helix protein — the structural scaffolding in skin, bones, tendons, and connective tissue. A single collagen molecule weighs roughly 300,000 daltons. Your gut can absorb molecules up to about 10,000 daltons. The maths doesn't work: eat intact collagen and your digestive system treats it like any other large protein, breaking it down into individual amino acids with no particular collagen-specific benefit.

Hydrolysis solves this by using enzymes to cut the collagen chain into much smaller fragments — typically 2,000 to 5,000 daltons — before you eat it. These fragments are small enough to survive digestion partially intact, crossing the gut wall as short peptide chains rather than being fully dismantled into single amino acids first.

That matters because the research suggesting collagen supplements may have specific effects — the [VERISOL skin-elasticity trial](/guides/does-collagen-improve-skin-elasticity), the [joint-pain studies](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee), the [ARU umbrella review](/guides/the-biggest-collagen-study-yet) — all used hydrolysed collagen. The hypothesis behind why collagen might do something beyond what any protein source would do rests on these specific bioactive peptide fragments reaching the bloodstream.

## What actually survives digestion

This is the part worth understanding, because it's the mechanism behind every claim in the collagen supplement market.

When you eat hydrolysed collagen, specific dipeptides — particularly prolyl-hydroxyproline (Pro-Hyp) — have been detected in human blood after ingestion. A 2024 pilot study published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (Virgilio et al.) gave six healthy volunteers a single 10g dose of collagen hydrolysate and measured what appeared in their blood. Pro-Hyp was the most abundant peptide detected, appearing alongside Hyp-Gly, Gly-Pro-Hyp, and others. Between 36% and 47% of the absorbed hydroxyproline was still peptide-bound — meaning it hadn't been fully broken down into individual amino acids.

Separately, earlier research (Iwai et al., 2005) first showed Pro-Hyp appearing in human blood after oral collagen intake, and Ohara et al. (2010) demonstrated that Pro-Hyp stimulates fibroblast activity and hyaluronic acid synthesis in laboratory cell studies.

Two honest caveats. First, the Virgilio study had six participants — a pilot, not a large trial. Second, "detectable in blood" and "reaches skin in meaningful amounts" are different claims, and the step from cell studies to whole-body effects is where the evidence thins. But the mechanism isn't hypothetical — it's been traced, step by step, even if the clinical significance is still being established.

## "Hydrolysed collagen" vs "collagen peptides" — same thing, different label

If you've seen both terms and wondered which is better, the answer is neither, because they're the same product. "Hydrolysed collagen," "collagen peptides," and "collagen hydrolysate" are all names for collagen that's been enzymatically broken into small, absorbable fragments. The different labels are branding choices, not different ingredients or different processes.

Some products use one term, some use the other, some use both. Among the six products tracked in our [where-to-buy guide](/where-to-buy): [BB LAB](/where-to-buy), [FOODOLOGY](/where-to-buy), [Cloo9](/where-to-buy), and [HAMCHOROK](/where-to-buy) all use fish-derived hydrolysed collagen peptides. [NOW Foods](/where-to-buy) uses VERISOL — a patented bovine collagen peptide, the same one behind the most-cited elasticity trial. [YIXO](/where-to-buy) is the exception: it's a vegan product that doesn't contain collagen at all, using plant-based ingredients marketed as supporting the body's own collagen production instead.

If a label says "collagen peptides" without "hydrolysed," it's still hydrolysed — that's how peptides are made from collagen. If it says "hydrolysed collagen" without "peptides," it still contains peptides — that's what hydrolysis produces.

## Where gelatin fits in

Gelatin is the halfway house. It's collagen that's been partially broken down — heated and extracted, but not enzymatically hydrolysed into small peptides. Its fragments are larger (roughly 15,000 to 25,000 daltons versus 2,000 to 5,000 for hydrolysed collagen), which is why gelatin gels when it cools and hydrolysed collagen doesn't.

Gelatin is a food ingredient — it's in jelly, marshmallows, capsule shells. It's not what collagen supplement research uses, and it's not what "hydrolysed collagen" on a supplement label means. If you're comparing products and one says "gelatin" where another says "hydrolysed collagen peptides," they're meaningfully different — the gelatin product hasn't been processed to the same degree.

Worth noting: collagen jelly sticks use hydrolysed collagen peptides suspended in a jelly-like texture. The "jelly" part is the format, not the collagen source — the collagen inside is hydrolysed, the same as in a powder or liquid sachet. ## What about "Type I," "Type II," and "marine" vs "bovine"?

These are separate questions from hydrolysis, but they often appear on the same label and get confused with it.

**Type I, II, and III** refer to which collagen protein the product started with — Type I is the most abundant in skin and bone, Type II is found mainly in cartilage, and Type III appears alongside Type I in skin and blood vessels. Most supplements, including all the jelly sticks we track, use Type I. Our [ingredients comparison](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-benefits-and-ingredients) has the full breakdown by product.

**Marine vs bovine** is the source animal — fish versus cattle. Both can be hydrolysed to the same degree. The choice between them is about sourcing preference (dietary restrictions, sustainability concerns, halal/kosher considerations) rather than a fundamental difference in what hydrolysis does to them. Our [halal guide](/guides/are-collagen-jelly-sticks-halal) covers the dietary-compliance angle.

## The bottom line

"Hydrolysed collagen" on a UK label isn't a premium feature — it's the baseline processing that makes a collagen supplement function. A product that didn't hydrolyse its collagen would be selling you something your gut can't absorb in any collagen-specific way. The word tells you the manufacturer has done the minimum necessary step, not that they've done something special.

What actually varies between products — and what's worth comparing — is the [dose](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-benefits-and-ingredients), the [source](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-benefits-and-ingredients), whether the specific peptide has been studied (VERISOL is the standout example), and the [price you're paying per gram](/where-to-buy). "Hydrolysed" is the entry ticket, not the differentiator.
