Because your body genuinely can't make collagen without it. That's not a marketing line — it's one of the most thoroughly established facts in biochemistry, and the reason scurvy exists. Vitamin C is a required cofactor for the enzymes that stabilise collagen's structure. No vitamin C, no stable collagen, no matter how much collagen protein you eat.

That said, there's a meaningful gap between "vitamin C is essential for collagen synthesis" and "you need to buy a supplement that bundles both." Most of what the packaging implies sits in that gap. Here's what the science actually says, what the regulations allow products to claim, and what remains untested.

## What vitamin C actually does in collagen synthesis

Collagen is the most abundant protein in your body — it's the structural scaffold of skin, cartilage, bone, tendons, and blood vessels. But raw collagen protein, freshly assembled from amino acids, is unstable. It needs a chemical modification called hydroxylation before it can fold into the triple-helix shape that gives collagen its strength.

Two enzymes handle this: prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase. They attach hydroxyl groups to the proline and lysine residues in procollagen (the precursor form). Hydroxyproline, in particular, is what holds the triple helix together through hydrogen bonds. Without enough of it, the helix unravels at body temperature instead of staying locked in place.

Both enzymes require three things to function: iron, 2-oxoglutarate (an intermediate in your cells' energy metabolism), and ascorbic acid — vitamin C. Vitamin C's specific role is to keep the iron atom at the enzyme's active site in its reduced (Fe²⁺) form. If the iron oxidises to Fe³⁺, the enzyme stalls. Vitamin C resets it. This has been understood since at least the 1970s, confirmed across decades of biochemistry research, and thoroughly reviewed by Peterkofsky (1991) and more recently by Pullar, Carr and Vissers in their 2017 review in *Nutrients*.

This isn't a marginal contribution. It's a bottleneck. When vitamin C is absent, collagen synthesis doesn't slow down — it breaks.

## Scurvy: the proof that predates supplements by centuries

The most dramatic evidence for the vitamin C–collagen link is the oldest: scurvy. Sailors on long voyages without fresh fruit developed bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor wound healing, skin fragility, joint pain, and eventually death — all symptoms of collagen failing throughout the body.

James Lind's 1747 trial aboard HMS *Salisbury*, in which citrus fruit cured scorbutic sailors while other remedies failed, was one of the earliest controlled experiments in medicine. The biochemical explanation came two centuries later: without dietary vitamin C, prolyl hydroxylase can't function, procollagen can't be properly hydroxylated, and the collagen your body constantly turns over can't be replaced with structurally sound new collagen. Existing collagen degrades normally; the replacement collagen is defective. The result is a body whose connective tissue is progressively failing.

Scurvy is rare in the UK today — 40mg of vitamin C per day is enough to prevent it, and most people get more than that from food alone. But it proves, about as conclusively as medicine ever proves anything, that vitamin C and collagen synthesis are inseparable.

## The one claim products are actually allowed to make

This is where the mechanism meets the marketplace, and where the distinction matters.

The only UK/EU-authorised health claim connecting vitamin C to collagen is this:

> *Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin.*

The same wording applies with "cartilage," "bones," "gums," "teeth," or "blood vessels" substituted for "skin." All six variants are authorised under Commission Regulation (EU) No 432/2012, retained in UK law after Brexit.

Two words in that claim do a lot of work. **"Normal"** — meaning maintaining ordinary function, not enhancing, restoring, or reversing damage. And **"contributes to"** — meaning it plays a role in the process, not that supplementing with extra vitamin C will produce extra collagen. A product that implies more than this — that its vitamin C will "boost" collagen production or "supercharge" your skin — is going beyond what the regulation permits.

This is also a claim about vitamin C, not about supplemental collagen. The authorised wording says nothing about eating collagen peptides. It says vitamin C supports your body's own collagen-manufacturing process. That's why almost every collagen supplement includes it: without a meaningful vitamin C dose, the product can't lean on the only authorised claim in the category.

## Does adding vitamin C to a collagen supplement actually help?

This is the question most people assume has been answered. It hasn't — not directly.

The mechanism is real: your body needs vitamin C to make collagen. But the collagen supplement trials that this site covers — the [VERISOL skin-elasticity trial](/guides/does-collagen-improve-skin-elasticity), the [Clark et al. joint-pain study](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee), the trials reviewed in the [ARU umbrella review](/guides/the-biggest-collagen-study-yet) and the [Bassila et al. systematic review](/guides/25-studies-one-question-does-eating-collagen-reach-your-skin) — didn't isolate vitamin C as a variable. Some gave participants collagen with vitamin C; others gave collagen alone. None directly compared the two to see whether vitamin C addition made the collagen work better.

A 2024 randomised controlled trial (Žmitek et al., published in *Nutrients*, 87 women aged 40–65, 16 weeks) tested 5g of hydrolysed collagen plus 80mg of vitamin C against a placebo, finding significant improvements in dermis density, skin roughness, and wrinkle volume. But the comparison was against placebo, not against collagen without vitamin C. It tells us the combination did something; it doesn't tell us what the vitamin C specifically contributed beyond what the participants' normal diet was already providing.

That gap matters because the participants weren't vitamin-C-deficient. They were healthy women eating normal diets. Their prolyl hydroxylase was presumably working fine already. Adding 80mg of vitamin C on top of dietary intake may be genuine insurance, or it may be a belt alongside braces that were already holding.

## How much vitamin C do you actually need?

The NHS recommends 40mg per day for adults — enough to prevent deficiency and support normal function, including collagen synthesis. A medium orange contains about 50–70mg. A serving of red pepper, about 130mg. Strawberries, broccoli, blackcurrants, brussels sprouts, and potatoes all contribute.

Most UK adults comfortably exceed 40mg through food. The NHS advises that taking more than 1,000mg per day may cause stomach pain, diarrhoea, and flatulence — vitamin C is water-soluble, so excess is excreted rather than stored, but high doses can still cause digestive discomfort.

The vitamin C doses in collagen supplements vary. Some jelly sticks include it as a minor addition; the Elavate powder reviewed on this site lists 300mg of liposomal vitamin C per serving. Whether 40mg from your breakfast or 300mg from a supplement makes any difference to collagen synthesis in a person who isn't deficient is, again, an untested question. The mechanism has a floor (below which collagen synthesis fails), not a proven ceiling where more vitamin C produces more collagen.

## The practical takeaway

Vitamin C and collagen synthesis are genuinely linked — that's biochemistry, not marketing. Your body cannot make stable collagen without it, and scurvy is the centuries-old proof. The EU/UK-authorised health claim reflects this real mechanism, and products include vitamin C partly because the science supports it and partly because the regulation requires it for the claim.

But "your body needs vitamin C to make collagen" is not the same as "you need to buy vitamin C bundled with your collagen supplement." If your diet includes fruit and vegetables — and most UK diets do — you're already providing the cofactor your enzymes need. The vitamin C in a supplement is a reasonable safety margin, not a proven performance enhancer, and no trial has shown that supplemental vitamin C makes supplemental collagen work better than it would alongside a normal diet.
