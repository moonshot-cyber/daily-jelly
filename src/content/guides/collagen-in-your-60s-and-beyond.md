The joint and muscle evidence for older adults is real and age-specific — not a rehash of the skin story used to sell collagen to a younger audience. A 2025 trial found meaningful knee-pain improvement at 3,000mg/day; a study specifically in elderly men found real muscle gains at 15g/day alongside resistance training. Both doses are well above what a single jelly stick delivers, and neither result is about skin.

## Why this isn't the same claim as "starting collagen in your 30s"

Most of the collagen-and-age content on this site — and everywhere else — circles back to the same honest answer: there's no evidence for an ideal starting age, and consistency matters more than timing. That's still true here. What's different for the 60s-and-beyond bracket specifically is that there's genuine, age-relevant clinical evidence for outcomes that have nothing to do with skin — joint pain and muscle mass, both of which become more clinically relevant later in life regardless of whether you've been taking a supplement for one year or twenty.

## Joint pain: real evidence, and it's Type I

A double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* in 2025 gave 80 adults aged 40–75 with early-stage (Kellgren–Lawrence grade I–II) knee osteoarthritis either 3,000mg/day of low-molecular-weight collagen peptides or a placebo, for 180 days. The peptides were sourced from fish skin and are explicitly Type I collagen — the same type used in every jelly stick we track, [not the separate Type II (UC-II) category](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-benefits-and-ingredients) sometimes marketed for joints at very different, milligram-level doses. The trial found significant improvement in WOMAC pain and function scores in the collagen group versus placebo.

Worth being clear about who this evidence applies to: adults aged 40–75 with early-stage osteoarthritis specifically, not a general "collagen helps joints as you age" claim. It's a real, well-designed result in a genuinely age-relevant population — but 3,000mg/day is roughly the top end of what one of our featured sticks delivers, sustained daily for six months, not a single serving.

## Muscle and strength: real evidence, but only shown in men so far

A randomised controlled trial in *British Journal of Nutrition* gave 53 elderly sarcopenic men, mean age 72.2, either 15g/day of collagen peptides or a placebo, combined with a 12-week supervised resistance training programme. The collagen group gained significantly more fat-free mass (+4.2kg vs +2.9kg), more strength (+16.5Nm vs +7.3Nm), and lost more fat mass than the placebo group.

Two honest caveats. First, this trial was conducted in men only — we couldn't find an equivalent, similarly-designed trial in older women, so this specific result doesn't tell you anything about whether the same holds for women in the same age bracket. Second, the collagen was paired with actual resistance training throughout; nothing here suggests collagen alone, without training, produces the same effect.

## Bone density: one genuine positive result, in a narrower population

A randomised controlled trial in 102 postmenopausal women found that a year of daily specific collagen peptides was associated with improved bone mineral density T-scores at the spine and femoral neck, alongside a favourable shift in bone-formation markers compared with control. That's a real, positive result — but it's the newest and thinnest of the three findings here: one trial, in postmenopausal women specifically (not men, and not a broader older-adult population), where other bone-density research on collagen has produced more mixed results. We're reporting it because it's genuine evidence, not because it's as well-established as the joint or muscle findings above.

## What this doesn't mean for skin

None of the above is a reason to expect better skin results later in life. The skin evidence cited in [our evidence roundup](/guides/do-collagen-jelly-sticks-actually-work) — the VERISOL trial — was conducted in women aged 35–55, not an older population, and skin-elasticity claims aren't what the 60s-and-beyond evidence is actually about. If skin is your reason for taking collagen, that evidence doesn't change with age; it's the same trial, the same caveats, regardless of how old you are when you start.

## What you're actually allowed to claim

None of this is a health claim, and we're not making one. In the UK and EU, the only authorised claim connecting a nutrient to collagen is that **Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin**. The joint, muscle, and bone trials above are real, published, peer-reviewed research, reported honestly — not a claim that any product on this site "treats" osteoarthritis, "builds" muscle, or "prevents" bone loss. The doses used in these trials (3g to 15g daily, sustained for months) are well above what a single jelly stick provides, so don't expect one stick a day to replicate a clinical trial result.

## The honest verdict

If joint pain or age-related muscle loss is why you're considering collagen, there's genuine evidence worth knowing about — more specific and more age-relevant than the general skin story most marketing leans on. It's not a substitute for what actually treats those conditions, and the doses involved mean a single stick is a modest daily contribution, not a clinical dose.
