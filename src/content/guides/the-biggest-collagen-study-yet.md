Nearly 8,000 people across 113 randomised controlled trials, pulled together in one umbrella review. It's the largest integrated analysis of collagen supplementation published to date — and its conclusion is more useful than a simple yes or no: collagen has credible benefits for skin when used consistently over time, it's not a cure-all, and the marketing usually runs ahead of the evidence.

That's a more honest starting point than most of the internet gives you.

## What the review actually covered

Published in *Aesthetic Surgery Journal Open Forum* in 2026, the umbrella review was led by Professor Lee Smith at Anglia Ruskin University and Dr Roshan Ravindran at KLNIK. Unlike a single trial or even a regular systematic review, an umbrella review analyses *other systematic reviews* — 16 of them in this case, spanning 113 RCTs and nearly 8,000 participants from around the world. It covered skin, joints, muscles, tendons, metabolic health, and oral health — the full range of what collagen supplements are marketed for.

That breadth is what makes it useful. Instead of one trial in one population measuring one thing, this asks: across everything we've studied, where does the evidence actually hold up?

## Where the evidence holds up

**Skin:** Longer supplementation was associated with greater improvements in skin hydration and elasticity. This is consistent with what the site's own [evidence roundup](/guides/do-collagen-jelly-sticks-actually-work) already reports from the VERISOL trial and the UCLH summary — the ARU review just confirms it at a much larger scale.

**Osteoarthritis:** Clear relief from symptoms, with longer use linked to less pain and stiffness — which tracks with the joint-pain evidence covered in both the [runners' knee piece](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee) and the [collagen in your 60s](/guides/collagen-in-your-60s-and-beyond) guide.

**Muscle and tendon structure:** Modest improvements in muscle mass and tendon composition, framed by the authors as possible support for healthy ageing rather than a performance claim.

## Where it doesn't

**Sports performance:** No meaningful improvements in post-exercise muscle recovery, muscle soreness, or tendon mechanical properties. The researchers were direct about this — collagen should not be seen as a fast-acting sports performance supplement. If you're buying it for gym recovery, this review doesn't support that.

**Metabolic and oral health:** Evidence on cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar, gum disease, and dental outcomes was mixed or inconclusive. Not enough to recommend collagen for any of those purposes.

## The caveats worth reading

The review's own authors don't oversell it, and neither should anyone else:

- Benefits were **moderate rather than dramatic**. Nobody in 113 trials had a miraculous transformation.
- The evidence depends on **consistent, long-term use** — not a week of jelly sticks before a holiday.
- More high-quality trials are needed, especially on **long-term outcomes, optimal dosing, and differences between collagen sources**. Marine collagen, bovine collagen, and different peptide formulations aren't interchangeable just because they share a label.
- Newer trials tended to show stronger results — which may reflect better formulations and study design, or may reflect publication bias. The authors note the trend without claiming to know which it is.
- As Professor Lee Smith put it: "Collagen is not a cure all, but it does have credible benefits when used consistently over time."

## A second review landed the same year — and agrees on the important parts

A separate meta-analysis by Batool et al., published in the *Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology* in 2026, reached strikingly similar conclusions through a different analytical approach. It found meaningful improvements in hydration (from as early as 4 weeks) and elasticity (mainly at 12 weeks), but — crucially — **did not find robust evidence that oral collagen reduces wrinkles or increases dermal density**. Those are the two claims supplement marketing leans on hardest, and they're the two that didn't survive rigorous analysis.

One other finding from that review matters: **treatment duration appeared to be more important than dose.** Their meta-regression found no significant linear dose-response relationship — meaning there's no evidence that doubling the dose doubles the benefit. Consistency over weeks mattered more.

Two independent research groups, different methods, same year, same core message: real but moderate benefits for hydration and elasticity with consistent use; weak or absent evidence for wrinkle reduction; and marketing claims that consistently outpace what the trials actually show.

## What this means for jelly sticks specifically

Nothing about this review changes the format question — a jelly stick delivers collagen the same way a powder, liquid sachet, or gummy does, and the evidence applies (or doesn't) regardless of format. What it does reinforce is that **consistency matters more than dose**, which is actually the strongest practical argument for a format you'll actually take every day. If you'd skip a powder but eat a jelly stick, the format might matter — not because it changes the biology, but because it changes whether you keep showing up.
