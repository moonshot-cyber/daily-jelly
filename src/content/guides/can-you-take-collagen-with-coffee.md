Yes, you can. The worry behind the question — that hot coffee "denatures" the collagen and wastes your money — rests on a misunderstanding of what's actually in a collagen supplement. Hydrolysed collagen has already been taken apart before it reaches you, so there's no delicate structure left for a hot drink to ruin, and the temperatures that would genuinely degrade it sit hundreds of degrees above anything you'd drink.

It's also, mostly, a powder-drinker's question. A jelly stick never goes near your coffee. But there is a more interesting question hiding underneath this one, and it's about the caffeine rather than the heat — where the evidence is thinner and stranger than either the worriers or the reassurers tend to admit.

## The worry, and why it's aimed at the wrong thing

"Denaturing" a protein means destroying its three-dimensional folded shape — the weak bonds that hold a protein in a particular configuration come apart, and it loses the structure its function depended on. Heat does this readily. It's what happens to an egg white in a pan.

Collagen in your body is a triple helix: three protein chains wound around each other. That structure can certainly be destroyed by heat. The problem with the coffee worry is that in a collagen supplement, it already has been — deliberately, by the manufacturer, before the tub was sealed. As our guide to [what "hydrolysed collagen" means on a UK label](/guides/what-hydrolysed-collagen-means-on-a-uk-label) sets out, the hydrolysis step enzymatically chops collagen from a molecule of roughly 300,000 daltons into peptides of around 2,000–5,000 daltons. That's the whole point of the process: the fragments are small enough to be absorbed, which intact collagen is not.

You can't denature something that's already been dismantled. What's left in the sachet is short chains of amino acids, and the peptide bonds holding those chains together are far more robust than the weak interactions that gave the original protein its shape. Whatever a hot drink does to a collagen peptide, it isn't "denaturing" it, because that already happened in a factory.

Worth saying plainly, because it cuts both ways: the claim that heat breaks collagen down is not a myth. It's true, it's well understood, and it's industrially useful — heating collagen in water is how gelatin is made. The error is in thinking that process is still waiting to happen in your kitchen.

## What temperature would actually be needed

The honest version of the question is whether the amino acids and short peptides in the powder survive sitting in a hot liquid. Here the evidence is narrower than you'd expect, and worth describing precisely rather than overstating.

A 2018 study by Weiss and colleagues in *BMC Biophysics* measured the thermal decomposition of eight amino acids, including glycine — which makes up roughly a third of collagen's amino acid residues. Using differential scanning calorimetry, they found decomposition peaks between 185°C and 280°C, with glycine's at 250°C. Coffee is brewed somewhere around 90–96°C and generally drunk nearer 60–70°C.

Three caveats, because the gap between that study and your mug is real. The samples were dry powders heated under argon on a steady temperature ramp, not amino acids dissolved in hot water. The figures are peak decomposition temperatures, not the temperature at which decomposition begins, which is lower. And the study looked at free amino acids — not peptides, and not collagen.

So it bounds the question rather than closing it: the temperatures at which these molecules fall apart under laboratory conditions are an order of magnitude above drink temperature. What we could not find is the study people actually want, in which collagen peptides are held in water at 70°C for half an hour and someone measures what's left. If that work exists, we haven't located it, and we'd rather say so than fill the gap with a confident guess.

## The question worth asking instead

Set the heat aside and a better question appears: does caffeine itself do anything to collagen?

There is research here, and it initially looks alarming. A 2014 study by Donejko and colleagues in *Drug Design, Development and Therapy* added caffeine to cultured human skin fibroblasts — the cells that manufacture collagen — and measured how much collagen they produced over 24 hours. Collagen biosynthesis fell by about 48% at 1mM caffeine, 73% at 2mM, and 92% at 5mM, alongside reduced activity of prolidase, an enzyme involved in recycling proline for collagen synthesis. The authors proposed impaired β1-integrin and IGF-I receptor signalling as a mechanism, though they describe that as a possibility rather than something they demonstrated.

Read quickly, that sounds like a reason to keep caffeine away from anything collagen-related. Read properly, it isn't.

The lowest concentration at which that study found any effect on collagen production is more than twice a blood concentration considered lethal in a human being. Not "more than you'd get from a coffee" — more than a person survives.

Two honest qualifications on our own table. Converting between the concentration of a compound in cell-culture medium and its concentration in blood is not a like-for-like comparison, and skin cells in a living person aren't bathed in caffeine at plasma concentration anyway. The point isn't a precise equivalence; it's the order of magnitude, which is roughly fifty- to four-hundred-fold. And the study was measuring fibroblasts making new collagen — not a collagen supplement being digested and absorbed, which is a different process entirely. Nothing in it involved coffee, a drink, or a supplement.

This is the ordinary limitation of cell-culture work, and it's why we don't treat it as consumer advice in either direction. It is a legitimate finding about caffeine and skin cells at high concentrations. It is not evidence that your morning coffee undermines your collagen, and anyone citing it that way hasn't checked the numbers.

## Where the format actually comes in

All of this is a question for people who mix a powder into a drink. If you take a [jelly stick](/guides/jelly-sticks-vs-gummies-powders), the question dissolves: you tear the sachet, eat it, and drink your coffee whenever you like. There's no mixing step and nothing to weigh up.

That isn't an argument for switching formats — the heat worry was never a good reason to avoid a powder, so solving a non-problem isn't a selling point. It's simply worth knowing that the question only applies to one of the three main formats, and that the [format you'll actually stick with](/guides/best-time-of-day-to-take-collagen) matters a great deal more than what you wash it down with. Consistency over weeks is the one timing variable the evidence genuinely supports.

## What you're actually allowed to claim

None of this is a health claim, and we're not making one. In the UK and EU, the only authorised claim connecting a nutrient to collagen is that **Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin**. The studies above are real, published research, reported with their actual limitations — a cell-culture study on caffeine and a thermal-analysis study on dry amino acids are not evidence that any product on this site does anything, and nothing here is advice about caffeine intake.

## The honest verdict

Put the collagen in your coffee if that's what gets you taking it daily. The heat objection misidentifies what's in the sachet, the temperatures involved aren't close to the ones that would matter, and the caffeine research most often waved at this question was conducted at concentrations that would kill the person drinking it. The gap in the evidence — nobody appears to have tested collagen peptides held in a hot drink specifically — is real, and we'd rather name it than paper over it, but it's a long way from a reason to change what you're doing.
