## A different question from "collagen for joint pain" generally

Most "best collagen for joints" content lumps everything together — ageing, osteoarthritis, general "joint support" — into one buying guide, usually pointing at Type II (UC-II) products dosed in milligrams rather than the Type I collagen every jelly stick we track actually contains. That's not what this piece is about, and it's a different question from [collagen in your 60s and beyond](/guides/collagen-in-your-60s-and-beyond), which covers osteoarthritis in an older population specifically — see our [collagen guide hub](/collagen) for the wider category picture. This is about a younger, physically active group — people whose knees ache after training, not people with a diagnosed joint condition — and the trials behind it studied that population specifically.

## The evidence

A 2008 study at Penn State gave 147 athletes (72 male, 75 female) with activity-related joint pain and no evidence of joint disease either 10g/day of liquid collagen hydrolysate or a placebo, over 24 weeks. Pain, mobility, and inflammation scores all improved significantly more in the collagen group.

More recent research has settled on a lower dose. A randomised controlled trial published in *Nutrients* gave 180 physically active 18-30 year olds with exercise-related knee pain — again, no diagnosed joint disease — either 5g/day of specific collagen peptides, explicitly derived from Type I collagen, or a placebo, for 12 weeks. Exercise-induced knee pain fell significantly more in the collagen group (-21.9mm vs -15.6mm on a pain scale, physician-assessed p=0.003). This isn't a single isolated result either — several other independently-run trials in similarly active populations, using the same 5g/12-week protocol, have reported consistent pain reductions versus placebo. Worth the same honest caveat as anywhere else on this site: these are industry-funded trials of one specific collagen peptide ingredient, not independent replication from unrelated labs — a real, repeated finding, not a single fluke, but not the same as fully independent confirmation either.

## Who this evidence doesn't apply to

Every trial behind this specifically excluded people with a diagnosed joint disease or injury — the participants had ordinary, activity-related discomfort, not a diagnosed problem. If you've got a real knee injury, a diagnosed condition, or pain that isn't just the ordinary ache of training, this evidence doesn't speak to that, and a supplement isn't the next step — a physiotherapist or GP is.
