Absolute Collagen is a Birmingham-based brand best known for liquid marine collagen sachets, and it now sells the same 8,000mg dose as a powder. This guide sets out what the brand's own label and product page say about the powder sachets, works out the price per serving, and checks the brand's clinical claims against the trial paper it links to. **We haven't tested this product.** Everything below comes from the brand's pages as read on 27 November 2026, the published trial, and our own arithmetic. We have no first-hand experience of how it tastes or mixes, and nothing here is a verdict on whether it will work for you.

The page was running a Black Friday promotion when we read it ("Offer ends Monday"), so some prices below are temporary. We give the list price alongside.

## What the label says

The ingredients line on the product page reads: hydrolysed marine collagen (fish, 98.5%), vitamin C as calcium ascorbate, natural flavouring. Fish is shown in bold, as UK allergen labelling requires.

| Per daily sachet | Amount stated on the page |
|---|---|
| Hydrolysed marine collagen | 8,000mg |
| Vitamin C (as calcium ascorbate) | 60mg, given as 75% of the reference intake |
| Protein | 7.7g |
| Calories | 36 |
| Collagen type | Not stated (the page says "hydrolysed marine collagen") |
| Fish species, origin | Not stated |

We didn't find a full nutrition table on the page, so we can't give sugar, fat or salt figures. The page says the powder doesn't contain gluten, lactose or artificial additives, and that it's made on a line that also handles milk, soy, egg, celery, sulphites, molluscs and crustaceans.

Like most products we've checked, the label gives a collagen source but not a collagen type. Our guide to [Type I, II and III collagen](/guides/type-i-ii-iii-collagen-which-uk-products-use-which) explains why that's common and what fish collagen usually is.

## What the brand claims, and what its trial found

Absolute Collagen does something most brands we cover don't: it ran a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial and published it. The paper is Reilly et al., "A Clinical Trial Shows Improvement in Skin Collagen, Hydration, Elasticity, Wrinkles, Scalp, and Hair Condition following 12-Week Oral Intake of a Supplement Containing Hydrolysed Collagen", *Dermatology Research and Practice*, 2024. That's worth crediting. It's also worth reading the paper rather than the marketing, because the two don't always say the same thing.

**What the trial was.** Adults aged 40 to 60 (90% women) took one sachet of 8,000mg hydrolysed marine collagen plus 60mg vitamin C a day, or a matched placebo, for 12 weeks, in early 2023. A smaller group took a sachet every other day. 130 people completed it, 48 on the daily product and 52 on the daily placebo. The paper says sachet throughout and doesn't say whether the product tested was the liquid or this powder, which the page describes as new ("Now in powder form").

**What it measured, against placebo, at 12 weeks (daily group):**

| Measure | Result reported in the paper | Different from placebo? |
|---|---|---|
| Skin hydration (Corneometer) | +13.8% vs placebo (placebo fell over a winter trial) | Yes |
| Skin elasticity (Cutometer R2) | +22.7% vs placebo | Yes |
| Wrinkle depth (profilometry, Rz) | about −19.7% vs placebo | Yes |
| Collagen fragmentation (confocal, expert-graded) | −44.6% vs placebo | Yes |
| Dermal density (ultrasound, high-echogenic pixels) | +231% vs +176% on placebo | No |
| Total hair count (subset of 24 people) | +27.6% vs placebo | No |
| Self-rated wrinkle reduction | 62.5% vs 36.5% on placebo | No, per the paper |
| Hair "healthy appearance" grade | +31.9% vs 9.4% on placebo | Yes |

No adverse events were recorded.

**Where the marketing goes further than the paper.**

- **"100% saw results on wrinkles."** The measured wrinkle result was a real between-group difference. But when participants were asked, self-rated wrinkle reduction was the one questionnaire item the paper says wasn't statistically significant.
- **"27% increase in hair strands."** That's the hair-count figure, from 24 people, and the paper marks it as not significant.
- **"60% increase in skin elasticity."** We couldn't find that figure in the paper's main results table, where the elasticity gain against placebo is 22.7% at 12 weeks.
- **"8000mg collagen — the clinically proven dose."** The trial tested 8,000mg against nothing. It didn't compare doses, so it can't show 8,000mg is better than less. The every-other-day group, averaging about half the dose, saw a similar elasticity gain to the daily group (the difference wasn't significant), though that group was small.
- **"Guarantees results."** That's a refund promise (the page offers a 90-day money-back guarantee), not a finding.

**Who ran it.** The paper states that the trial was funded by Absolute Collagen, that all the authors except one work for Absolute Collagen, and that the trial was run on its behalf by Princeton Consumer Research in Chelmsford. The authors declare no conflicts of interest. We found no trial registration. None of that makes the results wrong, but it's one brand-funded trial without independent replication, and the size of the effect hasn't been confirmed by anyone else. That's the same caveat we apply to the industry-funded trials behind most collagen research, discussed in [the dermatologist's sceptical case](/guides/dermatologist-says-collagen-supplements-dont-work).

The one authorised UK/EU claim in this area is that vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin. At 60mg (75% of the reference intake), this product contains enough vitamin C to make it. Our guide to [why collagen and vitamin C are sold together](/guides/why-collagen-and-vitamin-c-are-sold-together) explains what that claim does and doesn't mean.

## Who should check the label

- **Fish allergy.** The collagen is fish-derived and the ingredients list bolds it. Our guide to [collagen and fish allergy](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-side-effects-fish-allergies) covers what the research does and doesn't show. An allergist, not a label, can tell you whether it's suitable for you.
- **Pregnant, breastfeeding, or taking medication.** The brand's own page says to consult a doctor first.
- **Anyone wondering about dose.** At 8,000mg a day, this sits inside the 2.5g to 15g range used across the trials we cover. Our guides on [how much collagen per day](/guides/how-much-collagen-per-day) and [taking too much](/guides/can-you-take-too-much-collagen) put that in context.

## How it compares with a jelly stick

Absolute Collagen's powder is a mix-in: the page suggests stirring it into tea, coffee, smoothies or yogurt. A jelly stick is eaten straight from the pack. At list price the powder works out cheaper per gram of collagen than any jelly stick we track, and it delivers more collagen per serving than most of them. If you want a stick format, our [best collagen jelly sticks in the UK](/guides/best-collagen-jelly-sticks-uk) and [where to buy](/where-to-buy) pages cover the options. If you're weighing sachets against sticks generally, see [jelly sticks vs liquid collagen sachets](/guides/jelly-sticks-vs-liquid-collagen-sachets).
