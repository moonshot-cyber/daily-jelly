Short answer: no, not really — but "the NHS doesn't recommend it" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and the actual reasoning is more useful than the headline.

## What an NHS trust actually says

[UCLH](https://www.uclh.org/resources/collagen/) (University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust) publishes a patient-facing resource specifically on collagen supplements, and it's a genuinely balanced starting point rather than a dismissal. It acknowledges "moderate evidence" that hydrolysed collagen peptides may slightly improve skin elasticity and hydration — it doesn't claim collagen supplements do nothing. But its central caution is blunt: **"the marketing often runs well ahead of the evidence."** The resource notes that trials in this space are often small, short-term, and industry-funded, and that reported improvements tend to be modest rather than dramatic. Its bottom line is that collagen supplements are "not a substitute for proven skincare basics such as sun protection" and a nutrient-rich diet.

That's a fair, evidence-led position — not "collagen is a scam," but "don't expect it to replace the fundamentals."

## What the NHS focuses on instead

Rather than promoting collagen supplementation, NHS-affiliated guidance on skin and nutrition consistently points to the building blocks your body needs to make its own collagen: protein from food (meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, soya) plus adequate vitamins and minerals. This is also where the one authorised UK/EU health claim comes from — **Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin** — which is why you'll see Vitamin C included in most collagen products, jelly sticks included. It's the one part of the collagen conversation with a properly authorised claim behind it, everything else about "boosting" or "restoring" collagen is marketing language, not approved health claims.

## The independent research context

This isn't just an NHS-specific view. A 2019 systematic review in the *Journal of Drugs in Dermatology* (Choi et al.) looked at the available randomised controlled trials on oral collagen and found preliminary, promising results for skin elasticity, hydration, and dermal collagen density — while explicitly calling for more research to establish optimal dosing. "Preliminary and promising, more research needed" is a genuinely different statement from "proven to work," and it's consistent with UCLH's more cautious framing rather than contradicting it. ## The honest summary

The NHS isn't out here recommending you buy collagen jelly sticks, and an NHS trust's own patient resource is explicitly wary of how the category is marketed. But that's a caution about overclaiming, not a verdict that oral collagen does nothing — the same trust resource acknowledges modest evidence for skin elasticity and hydration. If you're going to try one, do it with that framing in mind: a possible modest addition to your routine, not a substitute for diet, sun protection, or the basics.
