The honest answer: modest evidence supports specific, well-studied collagen peptides — like VERISOL — taken consistently over 8+ weeks, but the jelly-stick format itself makes no difference to whether any of it works. This is, by a clear margin, the question people actually type into Google about this format. Not "where can I buy one" — "does it work." Fair question. Here's an honest answer, not a marketing one.

## The format isn't the thing that "works" or doesn't

Worth separating two questions that get merged together: does *ingested collagen* do anything, and does the *jelly stick format specifically* do anything different from a gummy, powder, or liquid sachet containing the same collagen. The answer to the second is no — a jelly stick is a delivery format. Whatever evidence exists for oral collagen applies (or doesn't) regardless of whether you're eating it from a pouch, chewing it, or mixing it into water. Format changes convenience, taste, and how consistently people actually take it. It doesn't change the biology.

So the real question is the first one.

## What does the research actually show about collagen supplements?

A 2019 systematic review in the *Journal of Drugs in Dermatology* (Choi et al.) looked at the available randomised controlled trials on oral collagen supplementation and found preliminary but promising results for skin ageing — increases in skin elasticity, hydration, and dermal collagen density were reported across the reviewed studies, with a good safety profile and no significant adverse events. The review's own conclusion was that further research is needed to nail down optimal dosing, not that the case is closed.

One of the more specific, better-designed studies looked at VERISOL — the patented bioactive collagen peptide used in the [NOW Foods Collagen Jelly Beauty Complex](/where-to-buy) featured in our buying guide. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 66 women aged 35–55, an 8-week course of 2.5g daily VERISOL was associated with a 26% increase in skin hydration, a 9% improvement in skin elasticity, and a 25% reduction in wrinkle volume at the outer eye corner, compared with placebo.

That's a genuinely well-designed study with a real result. Two honest caveats: it's one peptide, from one manufacturer, and it doesn't automatically generalise to every collagen source in every product — marine collagen, bovine collagen, and "vegan collagen-support" blends aren't interchangeable just because they all say "collagen" on the pack.

## The more cautious view

[UCLH](https://www.uclh.org/resources/collagen/) (University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust) publishes a patient-facing resource on collagen supplements that's a useful counterweight: it acknowledges "moderate evidence" that hydrolysed collagen peptides may slightly improve skin elasticity and hydration, but is blunt that "the marketing often runs well ahead of the evidence" — trials are often small, short-term, and industry-funded, and reported improvements tend to be modest rather than dramatic. Their bottom line: collagen supplements are not a substitute for sun protection and a nutrient-rich diet.

That tracks with what the systematic review actually found too — "promising" and "preliminary," not "proven" and "dramatic."

## What you're actually allowed to claim

In the UK and EU, there's exactly one authorised health claim connecting a nutrient to collagen: **Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin.** That's it. Any product claiming to "boost collagen production," "reverse ageing," or "restore elasticity" is going beyond what's currently authorised — regardless of how convincing the packaging looks.

## The honest verdict

Modest evidence exists for specific, well-studied collagen peptides at meaningful doses over 8+ weeks — VERISOL is the clearest example, and it's one of the reasons we feature the NOW Foods product that uses it. Broader claims about anti-ageing miracles or dramatic transformation go beyond what the evidence currently supports. If you try one, a realistic expectation is "might help a little with hydration and elasticity, over weeks, alongside everything else you're already doing" — not "replaces skincare."
