If you've spent any time in a Korean pharmacy, convenience store, or an Olive Young beauty aisle, you'll have seen them: slim foil pouches, usually 20–30g each, sold in boxes of 10 to 30, sitting next to skincare rather than in a "supplements" section. That placement is the whole point. In Korea, these are treated as a beauty product you eat, not a pill you swallow.

## The format

A collagen jelly stick is exactly what it sounds like — a stick-shaped sachet filled with a soft, spoonable-but-drinkable jelly. You tear off the top and either squeeze it straight into your mouth or eat it with a small amount left in the pouch, like a fruit puree pouch for adults. No water, no swallowing a tablet, no mixing a powder into a drink.

Typical contents:

- **A collagen source** — usually low-molecular-weight fish (marine) collagen peptides, sometimes paired with a plant-based booster
- **A fruit concentrate** — pomegranate is common, along with peach, mango, and green grape, mostly for flavour but often marketed alongside the collagen story
- **Vitamin C** — added deliberately, because it's the one nutrient with an actual authorised claim tied to collagen (more on that below)
- **A gelling agent** — usually a food-grade thickener that gives the jelly its texture without needing refrigeration

## Why the format matters more than the ingredients

The collagen itself isn't new — collagen supplements have been sold in the UK for years as gummies, powders, and capsules. What's different is *how* it's taken. Korean "inner beauty" brands treat supplementation as a snacking habit: something you reach for the way you'd reach for a piece of fruit, not something you have to remember to take with a glass of water at breakfast. That's a habit-formation advantage gummies and powders don't have — no prep, no cleanup, shelf-stable, and small enough to carry in a bag or pocket.

## What you can and can't claim

UK and EU rules only permit one authorised health claim connecting a nutrient to collagen: **"Vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin."** That's it. Claims like "boosts collagen production," "reduces wrinkles," or "restores elasticity" go beyond what's currently authorised, regardless of what the packaging says in Korean or how the brand markets it overseas. If you're comparing products, the presence of vitamin C at a meaningful dose is the one ingredient with a legally backed reason to be there — everything else is taste, texture, and marketing.
