Most collagen products you can buy in the UK are Type I, sometimes with Type III, and very few labels make a point of it. We went through the labels and listings of the nine collagen products this site covers. Three gram-dose products state a type (Type I, or Types I and III). One product, a 40mg joint capsule, is Type II. Four give a collagen source but no type. One, the vegan jelly, contains no collagen at all.

That pattern is less worrying than it sounds. For hydrolysed collagen, the source usually tells you the type. Where the type really matters is the joint-supplement aisle, and those products state it clearly.

## What the three types are

Collagen isn't one protein. Ricard-Blum's review of the collagen family in *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology* (2011) counts 28 members in vertebrates, numbered I to XXVIII. Three of them turn up on supplement labels.

- **Type I** is the most abundant. A 2021 review of marine collagen in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (Xu et al.) puts it at more than 85% of human collagen. It's built from two different chains, α1(I) and α2(I).
- **Type III** sits alongside Type I. Ricard-Blum describes the collagen fibrils in skin as made "of collagens I and III".
- **Type II** is the collagen of cartilage, made of three identical α1(II) chains.

The type a supplement contains depends on what it was made from. Reviews of fish collagen (Silva et al., *Marine Drugs*, 2014; Jafari et al., *Polymers*, 2020) describe fish skin, scales and bone as sources of Type I collagen. Jafari et al. say collagen from fish cartilage "consists predominantly of type II". So a fish-skin collagen is a Type I product whether or not the box says so, and a Type II product has to come from cartilage.

## Reading the "not stated" rows

The three Korean jelly sticks without a stated type all list fish collagen. Going by the published reviews above, fish skin and scale collagen is Type I, so Type I is the reasonable assumption. It's still an assumption, made from the source, not from the label. We haven't tested any of these products in a lab, and none of the listings tells us which part of the fish was used.

Elavate is a less tidy case. Its label splits the blend by animal (bovine, marine, chicken) but not by type, and doesn't say which tissue the chicken collagen comes from. At 40mg of an 8,090mg serving, the chicken part is under 1% of the total either way.

## When the type actually matters

For gram-dose hydrolysed collagen (every jelly stick and powder in the table except the H&B capsule), the type is mostly a clue to the source tissue. Hydrolysis breaks the collagen into small peptides, which is why these products are dosed in grams. The skin trials we cover, such as the VERISOL study in our [skin elasticity guide](/guides/does-collagen-improve-skin-elasticity), used this kind of product. Type I versus Types I and III isn't the variable those trials were testing, so the evidence doesn't let you rank one above the other.

Type II is different. The H&B capsule is UC-II, undenatured Type II collagen at 40mg a day. It's processed to keep the collagen's native structure and is studied for knee osteoarthritis. The main trial behind it, Lugo et al. (2016, *Nutrition Journal*), is covered in our [joints vs skin guide](/guides/collagen-for-joints-vs-collagen-for-skin), along with its industry funding. It isn't a substitute for a gram-dose collagen, and a gram-dose collagen isn't a substitute for it. If a label says "Type II" but gives a dose in grams, it's worth checking whether the product is undenatured at all, because the UC-II evidence used 40mg of undenatured material.

## What this means if you're buying

- **Buying a jelly stick or powder for skin:** the type on the label is a minor detail. Source, dose and price per 1,000mg tell you more. Our [hydrolysed collagen guide](/guides/what-hydrolysed-collagen-means-on-a-uk-label) explains the label wording, and the [price index](/collagen-price-index) compares cost per 1,000mg.
- **Buying for joints:** check for "Type II" or "UC-II" and a milligram dose. None of the jelly sticks we track is that product.
- **Buying vegan:** there's no plant collagen of any type. Our [vegan guide](/guides/vegan-and-plant-based-collagen-jelly-sticks) explains what those products contain instead.
- **Avoiding fish:** the source matters more than the type. A fish-derived Type I and a bovine Type I are different allergens. See our [fish allergy guide](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-side-effects-fish-allergies).
