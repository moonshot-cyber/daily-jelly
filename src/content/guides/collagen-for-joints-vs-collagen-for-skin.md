Usually, yes. The collagen in a jelly stick or powder is a different type, a different dose, and works by a different mechanism from the collagen in a joint-specific capsule. That matters because supplement marketing often talks about "collagen" as though it's one thing — good for skin, hair, joints, bones, everything — when the clinical evidence behind each claim comes from different products studied for different outcomes.

Here's what the distinction actually is, and why it changes what you should expect from the product you're buying.

## The types that matter on a label

Your body contains at least 28 types of collagen. Three of them account for nearly all of it, and they're the ones you'll see on supplement labels:

**Type I** makes up roughly 90% of the collagen in your body. It's the main structural protein in skin, bone, tendon, ligaments, and teeth. When a supplement says "collagen peptides" or "hydrolysed collagen" without specifying a type, it's almost always Type I — and that includes every jelly stick and most powders on the UK market.

**Type II** is the dominant collagen in articular cartilage — the smooth, cushioning tissue at the ends of bones where they meet in a joint. It's a small fraction of total body collagen, but in cartilage specifically it's the main structural component.

**Type III** is often found alongside Type I in skin, blood vessels, and organs. Some supplements list "Type I and III" together, since they naturally co-occur in the same tissues.

The distinction isn't academic. The supplement industry sells Type I for skin and general health, and Type II — specifically in an undenatured form — for joint cartilage. They aren't interchangeable, and the evidence behind each one comes from separate trials.

## What jelly sticks and most powders actually contain

Every jelly stick we track on this site — BB LAB, HAMCHOROK, Cloo9, FOODOLOGY, NOW Foods, YIXO — contains Type I collagen, either fish-derived (marine) or bovine. The collagen is [hydrolysed](/guides/what-hydrolysed-collagen-means-on-a-uk-label/): enzymatically broken down from large 300,000-dalton molecules into small peptides of roughly 2,000–5,000 daltons, which are absorbed in the gut and circulate as bioactive di- and tripeptides like Pro-Hyp.

These products are dosed in grams — typically 1–5g per stick, depending on the brand. The clinical trials that found [skin benefits](/guides/does-collagen-improve-skin-elasticity/) used similar Type I hydrolysed collagen at doses from 2.5g (the VERISOL trial) to 10–15g (muscle and joint studies). The mechanism, as far as the evidence describes it, is that these small peptides reach dermal fibroblasts and stimulate collagen synthesis — a substrate-supply model.

This is the category the [evidence roundup](/guides/do-collagen-jelly-sticks-actually-work/) and the [how-much-per-day guide](/guides/how-much-collagen-per-day/) cover.

## The joint-specific category: UC-II

Undenatured Type II collagen — branded as UC-II — is a completely different product. It comes from chicken sternum cartilage, processed at low temperature to preserve the collagen's native triple-helix structure rather than breaking it down into peptides.

The dose is dramatically smaller: 40mg per day, not grams. That isn't a weaker product delivering less — it works by an entirely different mechanism. UC-II acts through oral immune tolerance: it's processed by Peyer's patches in the small intestine, which generate regulatory T-cells that modulate the immune response to Type II collagen in joint cartilage. The proposed effect is a reduction in the inflammatory processes that degrade cartilage, rather than supplying raw material for the body to build with.

The best-designed trial behind UC-II is Lugo et al. (2016, *Nutrition Journal*) — a multicentre, randomised, double-blind study that gave 191 people with knee osteoarthritis either UC-II (40mg/day), glucosamine plus chondroitin (1,500mg + 1,200mg/day), or placebo for 180 days. UC-II significantly outperformed both placebo (p=0.002) and the glucosamine-chondroitin combination (p=0.04) on total WOMAC scores — measuring pain, stiffness, and physical function. Notably, glucosamine-chondroitin did not significantly beat placebo in this trial.

The honest caveats: the trial was funded by InterHealth Nutraceuticals (now Lonza), the UC-II manufacturer, and two of the three authors were company employees. It was conducted at 13 centres in India, not in a UK or European population. And it's studying osteoarthritis — a degenerative joint condition — not general joint comfort or the activity-related discomfort covered in the [runners' knee evidence](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee/).

## The evidence sits on different shelves

This is where the "collagen for joints" marketing gets misleading. The skin evidence and the joint evidence don't come from the same products:

| What was studied | Collagen type | Form | Dose | Population | Key trials |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skin hydration, elasticity | Type I | Hydrolysed peptides | 2.5–10g/day | Women 35–55 | Proksch et al. (VERISOL), Batool et al. meta-analysis |
| Knee osteoarthritis | Type II | Undenatured (UC-II) | 40mg/day | Adults 40–75 with OA | Lugo et al. 2016 |
| Activity-related joint pain | Type I | Hydrolysed peptides | 5–10g/day | Active adults 18–30 | Clark et al. 2008, *Nutrients* RCT |
| Muscle mass, strength | Type I | Hydrolysed peptides | 15g/day | Elderly men | Zdzieblik et al. |

A product containing Type I hydrolysed collagen can point to the skin trials and, honestly, to the activity-related joint-pain trials. But it can't point to the Lugo et al. UC-II trial for osteoarthritis — that studied a different product entirely.

Equally, a UC-II capsule can point to the OA evidence, but it can't claim the skin-elasticity or hydration findings — those used hydrolysed Type I peptides at 60 to 250 times the dose.

## Where it gets blurred: Type I for active joints

There is real, published evidence that hydrolysed Type I collagen helps with joint comfort — but in a specific, younger population with a different problem from osteoarthritis.

The Clark et al. 2008 trial (Penn State, 147 athletes, 10g/day, 24 weeks) and the *Nutrients* RCT (180 active 18–30 year olds, 5g/day, 12 weeks) both found significant reductions in [activity-related knee pain](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee/) versus placebo, using Type I hydrolysed collagen — the same kind found in jelly sticks. These participants were young, physically active, and had no diagnosed joint disease. The mechanism here isn't the immune-tolerance pathway of UC-II; it's likely related to peptide stimulation of cartilage and connective-tissue turnover during recovery from exercise.

This is a genuine finding, and it's the one place where a jelly stick's Type I collagen has joint-relevant trial support. But it's not evidence for osteoarthritis, and the two shouldn't be conflated.

## What this means if you're buying

If your concern is skin — hydration, elasticity, the outcomes most people think of when they hear "collagen supplement" — then Type I hydrolysed collagen is the category with trial evidence. That's what jelly sticks contain, and the [evidence roundup](/guides/do-collagen-jelly-sticks-actually-work/) covers the case for and against, including the [industry-funding concern](/guides/dermatologist-says-collagen-supplements-dont-work/) and the honest limitations.

If your concern is osteoarthritis or cartilage-specific joint health, the better-evidenced product is UC-II — a different type, a different dose, a different mechanism, and a different product category entirely. It's sold as capsules, not sticks or powders, and no jelly stick we track contains it.

If your concern is activity-related joint discomfort — training soreness, not a diagnosed condition — then Type I hydrolysed collagen does have relevant trial evidence at 5–10g/day, and the [runners' knee guide](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee/) covers it in detail.

The honest bottom line: "collagen" isn't one product, and the clinical evidence behind different claims comes from different types used for different things. Knowing which type a product contains — and which trials it can honestly cite — is the difference between a purchase that matches the evidence and one that doesn't.
