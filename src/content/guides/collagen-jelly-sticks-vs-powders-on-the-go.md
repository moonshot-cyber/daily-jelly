If your collagen powder habit only survives at home — because a tub, a scoop, and a shaker bottle don't really work on a train, at a desk, or in hand luggage — that's a genuine gap jelly sticks are built for. This isn't a rehash of our [full format comparison](/guides/jelly-sticks-vs-gummies-powders); it's specifically about what changes when you take your collagen routine out of the house.

## The travel-specific problems with powder

**It needs water and a container.** A powder scoop is only half a dose — you need something to mix it in and something to drink it with. That's fine at a kitchen counter, awkward on a commute, and a genuine faff mid-flight or mid-hike.

**It reads as a "substance" at security.** A tub of unlabelled white powder in hand luggage routinely gets a second look at airport security — even though it's obviously a supplement, it's the kind of thing that can mean an extra bag check. A sealed, individually wrapped jelly stick doesn't raise that question at all.

**Spillage risk.** A powder tub that isn't perfectly sealed in a bag is a genuine mess risk. A jelly stick pouch is flat, sealed, and takes up less room than a phone.

## Where this is actually going in the wider market

We'd stop short of saying jelly sticks are "replacing" powders — they're not; powder is still projected to be the single largest collagen format globally in 2026, at roughly 58% of the market. But the growth in this general space is real: Olive Young, South Korea's largest beauty retailer, reported "inner beauty" product sales (the category jelly sticks sit in, alongside liquid shots, gummies, and powders) up 24% among domestic shoppers and 49% among overseas shoppers in the first 11 months of 2025 versus the year before. That's a genuine, fast-growing category — just not yet a displacement of powder as the dominant format.
