Searching around the wider jelly-stick category, we came across a newer product we haven't featured anywhere else on this site: FOODOLOGY's Jeltiful PDRN Ampoule Jelly, sold on YesStyle, Stylevana, and Olive Young Global. It's marketed with more clinical-sounding language than anything else in this format — "ampoule," "skin booster," DNA-based repair — so it's worth explaining honestly rather than either dismissing it or repeating the marketing.

## What PDRN actually is

PDRN — polydeoxyribonucleotide — is a substance derived from DNA fragments, most commonly extracted from salmon or trout roe. It's a real, studied ingredient, not a marketing invention. In professional aesthetic medicine, it's used in "skin booster" treatments: injected into or under the skin by a practitioner to support tissue repair and wound healing, sometimes called (inaccurately, but commonly) "salmon DNA injections."

That's a genuine, evidence-backed clinical use — but it's an injectable, practitioner-administered treatment, not something you eat.

## The gap between injecting it and eating it

This is the part the marketing skips over: the research behind PDRN's tissue-repair effects is about direct injection into skin, not oral consumption. Eating a jelly stick means the ingredient passes through digestion before anything reaches your bloodstream — a completely different route into the body from an injection, with no established evidence that the same effects follow. We couldn't find research showing that ingested PDRN produces the skin-repair effects associated with the injectable form. That doesn't mean it definitely does nothing — it means the claim being implied (eat this, get skin-booster-style results) isn't supported by the same evidence being borrowed to sell it.

## Worth knowing: in some places, PDRN is classified as a medicine, not a supplement

In Italy, a PDRN-based product (Placentex®) is approved by the national medicines regulator as an actual pharmaceutical, for treating wounds and skin ulcers — a meaningfully different regulatory category from a food supplement. We couldn't confirm any UK or EU authorisation of PDRN as a novel food ingredient for oral use. That's not the same as confirming it's banned — food and supplement regulation in this area is genuinely fragmented across markets — but it's a reasonable prompt for caution rather than treating "it's in a jelly on a retailer's website" as proof it's been properly assessed for eating.

## The other claim on the listing worth flagging

The product's own listing describes "300 Da ultra-low molecular collagen, absorbed up to 200 times more efficiently than standard collagen." That's a striking number with no independent source behind it that we could verify — treat it the same way we'd treat any brand's own absorption claim: as marketing copy, not an independently confirmed fact.

## One more thing worth knowing about this brand

FOODOLOGY is the same brand behind the Cutting jelly and Bowel Movement jelly we've flagged elsewhere on this site — products explicitly marketed for weight management that we deliberately don't feature as collagen picks. One brand selling a wide spread of jelly products, from fibre snacks to this PDRN formula, doesn't mean each product carries the same level of evidence — worth checking each one on its own terms rather than assuming a brand's other products vouch for it.
