A 2025 trial at Pusan National University in South Korea found that adults with early knee osteoarthritis who took 3,000mg of fish-skin collagen peptides a day for six months reported less knee pain than those on a placebo. That's a real, properly controlled result. It's also narrower than the headlines suggest: one trial of 80 people, of whom 60 were analysed, with an industry partner making the tablets, and with several measures (a second pain scale, stiffness, X-ray joint space, inflammation markers) showing no difference between the groups at all.

This guide goes through what the trial did and what it found, and what it means if you're thinking about collagen for your knees. We've summarised it briefly before in [collagen in your 60s and beyond](/guides/collagen-in-your-60s-and-beyond); this is the full read.

## The trial in one paragraph

The study is Park et al., "Efficacy and safety of low-molecular-weight collagen peptides in knee osteoarthritis: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial", published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* on 4 September 2025 (volume 12, article 1644899, DOI [10.3389/fnut.2025.1644899](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1644899)). It ran at Pusan National University Korean Medicine Hospital and was registered in advance with Korea's Clinical Research Information Service (KCT0005507). It was covered in the trade press by [NutraIngredients](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2025/10/14/collagen-peptide-may-reduce-knee-pain-improve-function-in-early-osteoarthritis) in October 2025.

## Who was in it

- **80 adults randomised**, 40 to collagen and 40 to placebo, aged 40 to 75.
- **Early-stage knee osteoarthritis only**: Kellgren-Lawrence grade I or II on X-ray (the two mildest grades), with knee pain of at least 30mm on a 100mm pain scale.
- **Mostly women**: of the 60 people analysed, 27 of 29 in the collagen group and 28 of 31 in the placebo group were female. Mean age was about 52 to 54.
- **Not runners or athletes.** This is a general, mostly middle-aged, mostly female group with diagnosed early osteoarthritis. The trials in young, active people with training-related knee ache are a different body of evidence, covered in our [runners' knee guide](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee).
- **Excluded**: anyone with a BMI of 30 or more, inflammatory or secondary arthritis, past or planned knee replacement, or recent use of NSAIDs, glucosamine, chondroitin or joint injections.

One baseline imbalance is reported: more of the placebo group had the mildest grade of osteoarthritis (77% grade I against 52% in the collagen group). The authors say it was the only baseline difference.

## What they took

Three 500mg tablets twice a day after meals, so **3,000mg a day, for 180 days**. The collagen was enzymatically hydrolysed from the skin of *Pangasius hypophthalmus*, a freshwater fish, and the authors describe it as rich in Type I collagen, standardised to over 15% tripeptides including at least 3% Gly-Pro-Hyp. The placebo tablets were matched for weight, size, appearance and taste.

That makes it relevant to jelly sticks in two ways: it's fish-derived, like most of the sticks we track, and it's Type I, the same type every stick contains (see [collagen for joints vs collagen for skin](/guides/collagen-for-joints-vs-collagen-for-skin) for why that matters). It is not a jelly. Nobody has tested whether collagen delivered in a jelly stick behaves the same way as a tablet, and we're not assuming it does.

## What it found: the full results table

The main outcome was the change in the WOMAC pain score, a standard osteoarthritis questionnaire, from the start to day 180. Lower scores mean less pain or difficulty, so a negative change is an improvement. The figures below are the published per-protocol results at day 180; the table is ours, compiled from the paper.

| Measure (day 180) | Collagen group change | Placebo group change | Difference between groups? |
|---|---|---|---|
| WOMAC pain (main outcome) | −1.90 | +0.61 | **Yes** (p = 0.006) |
| WOMAC physical function | −4.10 | −0.71* | **Yes** (p = 0.035) |
| WOMAC total | −6.24 | −0.45 | **Yes** (p = 0.028) |
| WOMAC stiffness | −0.41 | −0.06 | No (p = 0.423) |
| Knee pain on a 100mm visual scale (VAS) | −17.28 | −21.77 | No (p = 0.299), both groups improved |
| Patient global assessment | −9.38 | +0.55 | No (p = 0.054), borderline |
| X-ray joint space width | No meaningful change | No meaningful change | No |
| Inflammation markers (ESR, hs-CRP) | No meaningful change | No meaningful change | No |

\*The paper's results table gives −0.71 for the placebo group's physical-function change; its abstract prints the same figure without a minus sign. We've used the table.

On the main pain measure, the gap between the groups also built over time: the between-group difference wasn't significant at day 45 (p = 0.099), was at day 90 (p = 0.046), slipped just outside significance at day 135 (p = 0.060), and was clearest at day 180 (p = 0.006).

## How to read that honestly

**The positive result is real, not a fluke reading.** The trial was randomised, double-blind and registered before it started, and the pre-specified main outcome came out in collagen's favour. Function and total WOMAC scores agreed with it.

**But the second pain scale didn't agree.** On the simple 0–100mm pain scale, both groups improved a lot, and the placebo group improved slightly more. That's a reminder of how big the placebo effect is in knee pain, and it means that how you measure pain changes the answer here.

**Nothing structural changed.** There was no difference in X-ray joint space, so this is a symptom result, not evidence that collagen rebuilt or protected cartilage. Six months may simply be too short to see that, and X-ray isn't a sensitive tool for early osteoarthritis, but either way the trial doesn't show it.

**The sample got smaller.** 80 people were randomised, but 60 were included in the main analysis: 29 of 40 in the collagen group and 31 of 40 on placebo. Dropouts were for personal reasons, surgery, use of other treatments, poor compliance with the tablets, and one adverse event. The authors also say no adjustment was made for testing multiple outcomes, and describe the study as exploratory.

**There's industry involvement.** The paper states that NEWTREE Co., Ltd. manufactured the tablets, and two of the seven authors list affiliations with NEWTREE and with EVERSPRING Co., Ltd. That's normal for collagen research (most trials are run with or funded by ingredient makers) and it doesn't make the result wrong, but it's the reason independent replication matters.

**Safety looked fine, with one oddity.** The abstract says no adverse events occurred; the results section reports one participant dropping out because of an adverse event, which NutraIngredients describes as mild hives that cleared on their own and weren't linked to the supplement. No serious problems were reported. The trial excluded anyone with a collagen hypersensitivity, and anyone with a fish allergy should read our [fish allergy guide](/guides/collagen-jelly-stick-side-effects-fish-allergies) before trying a fish-derived product.

## How it fits with the other knee evidence

This trial doesn't stand alone, which is what makes it worth knowing about. Different groups, at different doses, in different populations, have found a similar direction of effect on knee pain:

| Trial | Who | Daily dose | Duration | Result on knee pain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Park et al. 2025 (this trial) | 80 adults, 40–75, early knee OA | 3g Type I fish-skin peptides | 180 days | Better than placebo on WOMAC pain; not on VAS |
| *Nutrients* RCT (see [runners' knee guide](/guides/does-collagen-help-runners-knee)) | 180 active 18–30 year olds, no joint disease | 5g Type I peptides | 12 weeks | Less exercise-induced knee pain than placebo |
| Clark et al. 2008, Penn State | 147 athletes, no joint disease | 10g collagen hydrolysate | 24 weeks | Less activity-related joint pain than placebo |

None of these is large, and the populations barely overlap, so they don't add up to "collagen fixes knees". What they do suggest is that the effect on knee pain is more than one lab's finding, and that it shows up over months, not weeks, which matches what we found in [how long does collagen take to work](/guides/how-long-does-collagen-take-to-work).

## What it means for a jelly stick

The studied dose was 3,000mg a day, every day, for six months. Here's how that compares with the sticks we track that state a collagen dose per stick:

## What you're actually allowed to claim

This is a report of published research, not a health claim, and no product on this site treats osteoarthritis. In the UK the only authorised claim linking a nutrient to collagen is that **vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of** bones, cartilage and skin, among others (see [why collagen and vitamin C are sold together](/guides/why-collagen-and-vitamin-c-are-sold-together)). Nothing authorises a claim that collagen itself helps joints.

If you have knee pain, especially pain that's new, worsening or stopping you doing things, see your GP or a physiotherapist first. Osteoarthritis has established first-line treatments: [NICE's osteoarthritis guideline (NG226)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations) puts therapeutic exercise at the core of care, alongside weight management where relevant. A supplement would sit alongside that, not replace it, and if you take regular medication it's worth checking with a pharmacist first.

## The honest verdict

This is one of the better-designed collagen trials for joints: registered, double-blind, placebo-controlled, six months long, and using the same Type I, fish-derived collagen sold in jelly sticks, at a dose a single higher-dose stick can match. It found less knee pain on the main measure and no change to the joint itself. With 60 people analysed, an industry partner, and a second pain scale that didn't agree, it's a promising result that needs repeating, not a settled one.
